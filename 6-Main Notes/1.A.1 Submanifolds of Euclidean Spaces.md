## 1.1 Definition

A subset $M \subset \mathbf{R}^{n+k}$  is an _n-dimensional submanifold of class $C^{p}$ of $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$_ if, for any $x \in M$, there exists a neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ and a $C^{p}$ submersion $f: U \to \mathbf{R}^{k}$ such that $U \cap M = f^{-1}(0)$ (we recall that $f$ is a submersion if its differential map is surjective at each point).

## 1.3 Proposition

The following are equivalent:
i) $M$ is a $C^{p}$ submanifold of dimension $n$ of $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$.
ii) For any $x \in M$, there exist open neighbourhoods $U$ and $V$ of $x$ and $0$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ respectively, and a $C^{p}$ diffeomorphism $f: U \to V$ such that $f(U \cap M) = V \cap (\mathbf{R}^{n} \times \{ 0 \})$.
iii) For any $x$ in $M$, there exist a neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$, a neighbourhood $\Omega$ of $0$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, and a $C^{p}$ map $g: \Omega \to \mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ such that $(\Omega,g)$ is a local parametrization of $M \cap U$ around $x$ (that is, $g$ is an homeomorphism from $\Omega$ onto $M \cap U$ and $g'(0)$ is injective).

## Proof 
Uses implicit function theorem.

## 1.5 Proposition

Let $M \subset \mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ be an n-dimensional submanifold of class $C^{p}$. Let $U_{1}$ and $U_{2}$ be two neighbourhoods of $m \in M$. If $(\Omega_{1},g_{1})$ and $(\Omega_{2},g_{2})$ are two local parametrizations of $U_{1} \cap M$ and $U_{2} \cap M$ respectively, then $g_{2}^{-1} \circ g_{1}$ is a $C^{p}$ diffeomorphism from $\Omega_{1} \cap g_{1}^{-1}(U_{2})$ onto $\Omega_{2} \cap g_{2}^{-1}(U_{1})$.
### Proof 
We need to show that the map in question is:
1. Well-defined on the specific domains.
2. A bijection (one-to-one and onto).
3. A $C^{p}$ map.
4. Has a $C^{p}$ inverse.
A map satisfying (2), (3) and (4) is by definition a $C^{p}$ diffeomorphism.

#### 1. Setup and domains
By the definition of a local parametrization:
- $g_{1}: \Omega_{1} \to U_{1} \cap M$ is a homeomorphism, where $\Omega_{1} \subset \mathbf{R}^{n}$ is an open set. As a map into the ambient space $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$, $g_{1}$ is of class $C^{p}$.
- $g_{2}: \Omega_{2} \to U_{2} \cap M$ is a homeomorphism, where $\Omega_{2} \subset \mathbf{R}^{n}$ is an open set. As a map into the ambient space $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$, $g_{2}$ is of class $C^{p}$.

Let's define the map $\phi = g_{2}^{-1} \circ g_{1}$.
- Domain of $\phi$: For $\phi(x) = g_{2}^{-1}(g_{1}(x))$ to be defined, we first need $x \in \Omega_{1}$. The result, $g_{1}(x)$, must then be in the domain of $g_{2}^{-1}$, which is $U_{2} \cap M$. So, the domain of $\phi$ consists of all $x \in \Omega_{1}$ such that $g_{1}(x) \in U_{2} \cap M$. This is precisely the set $$
A = \{ x \in \Omega_{1} |  g_{1}(x) \in U_{2}\} = \Omega_{1} \cap g_{1}^{-1}(U_{2}).
$$
- Codomain of $\phi$: Let $y = \phi(x)$. Then $g_{2}(y) = g_{1}(x)$. Since $x \in A$, $g_{1}(x) \in U_{1} \cap U_{2} \cap M$. Thus, $g_{2}(y) \in U_{1} \cap M$. Since $y \in \Omega_{2}$ by definition of $g_{2}$, the codomain (or more precisely, the range) of $\phi$ is the set $$
B = \{ y \in \Omega_{2} | g_{2}(y) \in U_{1}\} = \Omega_{2} \cap g_{2}^{-1}(U_{1}).
$$
We also need to show that $A$ and $B$ are open sets.
- $U_{2}$ is an open set in $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$.
- The map $g_{1}: \Omega_{1} \to \mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ is continuous (since it is $C^{p}$).
- The preimage of an open set under a continuous map is open. Therefore, $g_{1}^{-1}(U_{2})$ is an open set in $\Omega_{1}$. Since $\Omega_{1}$ is open in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, $g_{1}^{-1}(U_{2})$ is also open in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$.
- $A = \Omega_{1} \cap g_{1}^{-1}(U_{2})$ is the intersection of two open sets, so it is open.
- A symmetric argument shows that $B = \Omega_{2} \cap g_{2}^{-1}(U_{1})$ is also an open set in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$.
#### 2. Bijection
We will show that $\phi: A \to B$ is a bijection by constructing its inverse. Let $\psi = g_{1}^{-1} \circ g_{2}$. The domain of $\psi$ is $B$ and its codomain is $A$, by the same logic as above.

Let's check if $\psi$ is the inverse of $\phi$:
- For any $x \in A$:
$$
\psi(\phi(x)) = \psi(g_{2}^{-1}(g_{1}(x))) = g_{1}^{-1}(g_{2}(g_{2}^{-1}(g_{1}(x)))) = g_{1}^{-1}(g_{1}(x)) = x
$$
  So, $\psi \circ \phi = \text{id}_{A}$.
- For any $y \in B$:
$$
\phi(\psi(y)) = \phi(g_{1}^{-1}(g_{2}(y))) = g_{2}^{-1}(g_{1}(g_{1}^{-1}(g_{2}(y)))) = g_{2}^{-1}(g_{2}(y)) = y
$$
  So, $\phi \circ \psi = \text{id}_{B}$.

Since $\phi$ has a two-sided inverse, it is a bijection from $A$ to $B$.

#### 3. Smoothness ($C^{p}$ property)
We need to show that $\phi: A \to B$ and its inverse $\psi: B \to A$ are both $C^{p}$ maps. Since the argument is symmetric, we only need to prove it for $\phi$.

The map $\phi = g_{2}^{-1} \circ g_{1}$ is a composition. We know $g_{1}: A \to \mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ is a $C^{p}$ map. The difficulty is showing that $g_{2}^{-1}$ is a $C^{p}$ map when its domain is restricted to a piece of the manifold. We will use the fundamental definition of a submanifold (Proposition 1.3).

Let $x_{0} \in A$ be an arbitrary point in the domain of $\phi$. Let $p_{0} = g_{1}(x_{0})$. Since $x_{0} \in A$, we have $p_{0} \in U_{1} \cap U_{2} \cap M$. Let $y_{0} = \phi(x_{0}) = g_{2}^{-1}(p_{0})$. 

Since $M$ is a $C^{p}$ submanifold, for the point $p_{0} \in M$, there exists an open neighbourhood $V \subseteq U_{2}$ of $p_{0}$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ and a $C^{p}$ diffeomorphism $F: V \to W$, where $W$ is an open neighbourhood of $0$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$, such that: $F(V \cap M) = W \cap (\mathbf{R}^{n} \times \{ 0 \})$.  This map "flattens" the piece of the manifold $V \cap M$ onto a piece of the standard subspace $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ within $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$.

Let $\pi: \mathbf{R}^{n+k} \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ be the canonical projection onto the first $n$ coordinates: $\pi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n+k}) = (z_{1},\dots,z_{n})$. This is a linear map and therefore $C^{\infty}$ (and thus $C^{p}$).

Now, consider the composition map $h = \pi \circ F \circ g_{2}$.
- This map is defined on an open subset of $\Omega_{2}$ around $y_{0}$. Let's call this subset $\Omega_{2}' = g_{2}^{-1} (V)$.
- $h: \Omega_{2}' \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$.
- $g_{2}$ is $C^{p}$, $F$ is $C^{p}$, and $\pi$ is $C^{p}$. Therefore, their composition $h$ is a $C^{p}$ map.

The Jacobian matrix of $h$ at $y_{0}$ is given by the chain rule:
$$
Dh(y_{0}) = D\pi(F(p_{0})) \cdot DF(p_{0}) \cdot Dg_{2}(y_{0}) 
$$
- $Dg_{2}(y_{0})$ is an $(n+k) \times n$ matrix of rank $n$ (since $g_{2}$ is a parametrization).
- $DF(p_{0})$ is an invertible $(n+k) \times (n+k)$ matrix (since $F$ is a diffeomorphism).
- $D\pi$ is the projection matrix which is an $n \times (n+k)$ matrix.

Let's trace a non-zero vector $v \in \mathbf{R}^{n}$ through his composition from right to left:
1. $w = Dg_{2}(y_{0})(v)$
   - $Dg_{2}(y_{0})$ is the differential of the parametrization $g_{2}$. It's a linear map from $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}.$
   - By definition of a parametrization, this map is injective (it has rank $n$). Its image is the $n$-dimensional tangent space to the manifold $M$ at the point $p_{0}$, which we denote $T_{p_{0}}M.$
   - Since $v \neq 0$ and the map is injective, its output $w$ is a non-zero tangent vector in $T_{p_{0}}M.$
2. $u = DF(p_{0})(w)$
   - $DF(p_{0})$ is the derivative of the diffeomorphism $F$. It's an invertible linear map from $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n+k}$.
   - Since it's invertible, it maps non-zero vectors to non-zero vectors. Because $w \neq 0$, the vector $u = DF(p_{0})(w)$ is also non-zero.
   - The map $F$ "flattens" the manifold $M$ onto the subspace $\mathbf{R}^{n} \times \{ 0 \}$. Its derivative, $DF(p_{0})$, therefore maps the tangent space $T_{p_{0}}M$ to the tangent space of $\mathbf{R}^{n} \times \{ 0 \}$, which is just the subspace $\mathbf{R}^{n} \times \{  0 \}$ itself.
   - So, $u$ is a non-zero vector of the form $(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n},0,\dots,0).$
3. Result $= D\pi(F(p_{0}))(u)$
   - $D\pi$ is just the projection map $\pi$ itself, which maps $(z_{1},\dots ,z_{n+k}) \to (z_{1},\dots,z_{n}).$
   - When we apply this to our vector $u$, we get $(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})$.
   - Since we know $u$ was a non-zero vector whose last $k$ components were zero, its first $n$ components cannot all be zero. Therefore, the result $(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})$ is a non-zero vector in $\mathbf{R}^{n}.$
This means that the map $Dh(y_{0})$ is injective. A linear map from $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ to itself that is injective is always invertible. That's why we can be certain the Jacobian is invertible.

Since $h$ is a $C^{p}$ map and its derivative is invertible at $y_{0}$, the Inverse Function Theorem implies that there exists an open neighbourhood of $y_{0}$ on which $h$ has a $C^{p}$ inverse, $h^{-1}$.

Now, we can express $g_{2}^{-1}$ locally. For any point $p \in V \cap M$, we have $F(p)= (z_{1},\dots,z_{n},0,\dots,0)$. Thus, $\pi(F(p))$ gives us the first $n$ coordinates.
For $y \in \Omega_{2}'$, we have $h(y)= \pi(F(g_{2}(y)))$. Applying $h^{-1}:y=h^{-1}(\pi(F(g_{2}(y))))$. 
So for a point $p = g_{2}(y)$ in the image, we can find $y$ by $y = g_{2}^{-1}(p)= h^{-1}(\pi(F(p)))$.

Thus, locally, around $p_{0}$, the map $g_{2}^{-1}$ can be written as the composition:
$$g_{2}^{-1} = h^{-1} \circ \pi \circ F$$
This expresses $g_{2}^{-1}$ as a composition of $C^{p}$ maps, so $g_{2}^{-1}$ is a $C^{p}$ map from the open set $V \cap M \subset M$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n}$.

Finally, we can analyse our transition map $\phi$ in a neighbourhood of $x_{0}$:
$$
\phi = g_{2}^{-1} \circ g_{1} = (h^{-1} \circ \pi \circ F) \circ g_{1}
$$
All the maps in this composition $(g_{1},F,\pi,h^{-1})$ are of class $C^{p}$. Therefore, their composition, $\phi$, is also of class $C^{p}$ in a neighbourhood of $x_{0}$. Since $x_{0}$ was an arbitrary point in $A$, the map $\phi$ is $C^{p}$ on its entire domain.

#### 4. Conclusion
We have shown:
1. The map $\phi = g_{2}^{-1} \circ g_{1}$ is well-defined from the open set $A = \Omega_{1} \cap g_{1}^{-1}(U_{2})$ to the open set $A = \Omega_{2} \cap g_{2}^{-1} (U_{1}).$
2. $\phi$ is a bijection, with inverse $\psi = g_{1}^{-1} \circ g_{2}.$
3. $\phi$ is $C^{p}$ map.
4. A symmetric argument shows that its inverse, $\psi$, is also a $C^{p}$ map.
Therefore, $\phi = g_{2}^{-1} \circ g_{1}$ is a $C^{p}$ diffeomorphism from $A$ onto $B$.
