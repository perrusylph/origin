## Exercise 1 (p.13) 
Fill in the details of the proof of Theorem 1.8. Also, prove the monotone decreasing case.
### Proof
In the proof of Theorem 1.8 we have this:
Suppose that $A_{n}$ is monotone increasing so that $A_{1} \subset A_{2} \subset \cdots$. Let $A = \lim_{ n \to \infty }A_{n} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_{i}$. Define $B_{1}= A_{1}, B_{2} = \{ \omega \in \Omega: \omega \in A_{2}, \omega \notin A_{1}\}$, $B_{3} = \{ \omega \in \Omega : \omega \in A_{3}, w \notin A_{2}, \omega \notin A_{1}\},\dots$ It can be shown that $B_{1},B_{2},\dots$ are disjoint, $A_{n} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_{i} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} B_{i}$ for each $n$ and $\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} B_{i} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_{i}$. Let's prove these assertions:
- First, let's take $B_{i}, B_{j}$ where $i\neq j$. Without loss of generality, $i<j$. Then, by definition, $B_{j}$ is the set of elements in $\Omega$ such that they are in $A_{j}$ but not in any $A_{k}$ such that $k<j.$ This means that there are no elements of $B_{j}$ in $A_{i}$. However, all elements of $B_{i}$ are in $A_{i}$ by definition. This means they are disjoint. The arbitrariness of $i.j$ lets us know all of the sets are disjoint. 
- It is clear that $A_{n} \subset \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}$ and by definition it follows that $\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i} \subset \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}$. Let's prove the missing containments. To see that $\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i} \subset A_{i}$ just note that since $A_{n}$ is monotone decreasing it happens that for any $i \in \{ 1,\dots,n \}$, $A_{i} \subset A_{n}$ and the union of subsets of a given set remains a subset of it. For the other containment, let $i \in \{ 1,\dots n \}$ and let $x \in A_{i}$. Then, we have two options: $x \in B_{i}$ or $x \notin B_{i}$. If we have the first, we are done. For the second option, it means that $x \in A_{k}$ for some $k<i$. Again we can check if $x \in B_{k}$ or not. If not, we continue the same process until we find some $m \geq 1$ such that $x \in B_{m}$. Note that if for every $m>1, x\notin B_{m}$ it will definitely be in $B_{1}$ since $B_{1} = A_{1}$.
- The equality of infinite unions follow from the fact that any $B_{i} \subset A_{i}$ and that if for some arbitrary $n$ we have $x \in A_{n} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i} \subset \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} B_{i}$.
Now let's prove the monotone decreasing case:

Suppose that $A_{n}$ is monotone decreasing such that $A_{1} \supset A_{2} \supset \cdots.$ Let $A = \lim_{ n \to \infty } A_{n} = \bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} A_{i}$. Define $B_{1} = \Omega - A_{1}, B_{2} = A_{1}-A_{2}, B_{3} = A_{2}-A_{3},\dots$ It's easily shown that $B_{1},B_{2},\dots$ are disjoint, that $A_{n} = \left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}\right)^{c} = \bigcap_{i=1}^{n}(B_{i})^{c}$ for each $n$ (using De Morgan's laws) and $\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}(B_{i})^{c} = \bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}$.
Using (1.1) and Axiom 3 we have:
$$
\mathbb{P}(A_{n}) = \mathbb{P}\left(\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}\right)^{c}\right) = 1- \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{P}(B_{i})
$$
and hence, using Axiom 3 and (1.1) again,
$$\begin{align}
\lim_{ n \to \infty } \mathbb{P}(A_{n}) &= \lim_{ n \to \infty } \left( 1-\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{P}(B_{i})\right) \\ &= 1-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(B_{i}) = 1- \mathbb{P} \left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} B_{i}\right) \\ &= \mathbb{P}\left(\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} B_{i}\right)^{c} \right) = \mathbb{P}\left( \bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty} (B_{i})^{c}\right) = \mathbb{P}(A)
\end{align}
$$
$\blacksquare$

## Exercise 2 (p.13)
Prove the statements in equation (1.1).
### Proof 
First, let's remember the three axioms of probability:
1. $\mathbb{P}(A) \geq 0$ for every $A$
2. $\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$
3. If $A_{1},A_{2},\dots$ are disjoint then $$
\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}).
$$
Let's prove then the statements in (1.1).
- $\mathbb{P}(\emptyset) = 0$. Given Axiom 1, we know that $\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)\geq 0$. Let's assume it's positive. Let's take this sequence of events: $A_{1}= \Omega, A_{2}= \emptyset, A_{3}= \emptyset\dots$ where $A_{i} = \emptyset$ for every $i\geq 2$. Then the union of this sequence gives this:$$
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i} = \Omega \cup \emptyset \cup \emptyset \dots = \Omega 
$$
It is also the case that $A_{1},A_{2},\dots$ are disjoint because of properties of intersection with the empty set.
Then, using Axiom 3 we have:$$
1=\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=\mathbb{P}(\Omega)+\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)+\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)+\dots
$$
This, in turn gives $$
1=1+\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)+\dots
$$
Which means that 
$$
\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}) =0
$$
However, for all $i\geq2, A_{i}=\emptyset$ and $\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)>0$ which makes the above series divergent because the terms inside are constants (the divergence test fails). This gives us a contradiction, which means that $\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)=0$.

- $A \cap B = \emptyset \implies \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A)+\mathbb{P}(B)$. Let the sequence $(A_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}$ be such that $A_{1} = A, A_{2} = B, A_{i} = \emptyset$ if $i>2$. Then, it is clear that the union of this sequence is $A \cup B$ and then, using axiom 3, the fact that the sequence is disjoint and that $\mathbb{P}(\emptyset)=0$, we have that 
$$
\mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B)
$$
